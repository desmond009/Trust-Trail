# Peer Reviewer 3 (Cross-disciplinary: data-protection law / privacy engineering)

Material read: main.tex, sections 00-13 and appendix (01, 02, 05, 06, 07 ledger/dashboard, 10, 11, 13 in detail), references.bib (law entries). Legal points were checked against my knowledge of GDPR and the DPDP Act 2023, plus a web check of the DPDP Rules 2025 (notified 13/14 Nov 2025) and the EDPB blockchain guidelines. I am not a cryptographer; I do not assess the Merkle/JWS details.

## 1. Summary

TrustTrail is a disclosure layer beside Keycloak: per-service pairwise pseudonyms, service-signed purpose declarations (attributes, purpose, deletion date), a hash-chained ledger of releases and refusals, Merkle-root anchoring on Ethereum, and a trust score that lowers a service's disclosure tier. The paper is candid about its main limit (no visibility after release) and its claim in 10-discussion is carefully worded ("supports the controller in demonstrating"). Technically-motivated privacy mechanisms are real: attribute-level minimisation by tier, per-service pseudonyms, and a student-visible record.

From a legal and governance standpoint the paper is weaker than its technical core. (a) The law is treated in one-line citations, with no mapping of mechanisms to specific provisions; the DPDP Act is described through GDPR vocabulary; roles (fiduciary, processor, data principal) and the legal basis for processing are never named. (b) The ledger itself is a long-lived store of pseudonymous personal data with no stated retention, erasure or access-correction path, which is the very storage-limitation problem the system claims to help with. (c) The claim that on-chain roots are harmless is asserted, not argued, and one specific design property (a ledger the operator "cannot change") collides with erasure. (d) Operator power is acknowledged but governance is a single sentence. (e) The ethics statement is inconsistent and the usability study has no consent/ethics design and recruits classmates of the authors.

## 2. Genuine strengths

- S-1 (10-discussion, "Evidence, not enforcement after release"; 05 non-goals): honest scoping. Says TrustTrail supports *demonstrating* purpose limitation/accountability and "does not prove storage limitation". This is the legally correct framing and avoids the common "GDPR-compliant" overclaim.
- S-2 (05-threat-model, 10 "What the operator can still do"): admits the operator can omit entries, hold the identity map, and reset trust; says anchors prove integrity-since-anchor, not truth.
- S-3 (04-related-work, para after blockchain designs): recognises the on-chain-erasure conflict, and keeps personal data off chain (only roots and ranges).
- S-4 (07-design 4.x dashboard): student can see only their own entries, verify, and download receipts. This is a practical transparency/access measure (cf. GDPR Art. 15, DPDP s.11 right to information about processing).
- S-5 (06-architecture, ledger): ledger holds attribute names, not values, and not the real Keycloak ID. Real data minimisation inside the evidence store.
- S-6 (07-design): signed declarations naming purpose and deletion date with refusal outside them is a concrete machine-checkable embodiment of purpose limitation at the point of disclosure.
- S-7 (13-backmatter): all data synthetic; AI-use disclosure present.

## 3. Weaknesses

### P-1 CRITICAL: Ledger and receipts are themselves personal data with no retention, erasure or correction regime
Location: 06-architecture (trusttrail.db description: "ledger contains ... pseudonyms and hashes"); 07-design "Entry format"; 10-discussion; 04-related-work ("ledger with its pseudonyms stays under the institute's control").
Problem: The paper treats the pseudonyms in the ledger as safe. Under GDPR Recital 26 / Art. 4(5) pseudonymised data stays personal data when the controller (here the same operator, who holds identity_map.db and the Keycloak salts) can re-identify. Under DPDP Act s.2(t) "personal data" is any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to such data; the operator can trivially identify. So the ledger records, per student and service, who accessed which attributes for which purpose and when: it is a processing log of personal data that is intended to be tamper-proof and permanent. The paper says nothing about how long entries are kept, how a student's entry is erased on graduation, withdrawal of consent, or an erasure request (GDPR Art. 17; DPDP s.8(7), s.12(3)), or how an incorrect entry is corrected (Art. 16; DPDP s.12(1)). A design whose headline property is "nobody can change history" directly collides with these rights. Ironically, the paper uses storage limitation as motivation (Intro, 02) while the evidence store has none.
Fix: Add a subsection "Data protection of the ledger itself" stating: legal basis/purpose of the ledger (accountability, legitimate use / legal obligation), a fixed retention period (e.g. enrolment plus N years), and a pseudonym-erasure design. A workable, honest design: derive the ledger's pseudonym field from a per-student key held in the identity map (or store a salted commitment), so that destroying the mapping (crypto-shredding) renders old entries unlinkable while the chain and roots still verify. State clearly that entry hashes then no longer verify for the erased student's content only if leaves are removed, or avoid this by hashing leaves over data that already excludes direct identifiers. Evaluate or at least discuss this trade-off.

### P-2 MAJOR: Anchoring and "are roots personal data?" is asserted, not analysed
Location: 04-related-work ("A root reveals nothing about the entries it covers"); 02-background (Anchoring "keeps the data off chain, which matters for personal data"); abstract ("without placing personal data on chain").
Problem: Hash outputs can be personal data when the hashed input is personal data and the means to link are reasonably available (CJEU Breyer C-582/14 on the "means reasonably likely" test; Recital 26; EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 on blockchain, which treat hashes and on-chain pseudonymous data with caution and recommend avoiding personal data on-chain, and note erasure is not achievable by deleting off-chain data alone unless it truly breaks the link). A single Merkle root over a batch of many entries, with no identifier and no salt-free low-entropy leaf accessible to outsiders, is very likely not personal data in the hands of outsiders, but the paper must show that, including: (i) batch size 1 or very small batches (low-traffic service, tamper experiment anchors "at least six entries" but nothing forbids n=1), where the root is a direct hash of one entry that an insider can verify for that one student; (ii) entry ranges and block timestamps reveal activity volume and timing; (iii) a student who downloads a receipt bundle holds a Merkle proof, so the leaf is verifiable against a public root by whoever holds the bundle. The unqualified phrase "without placing personal data on chain" in the abstract overstates the position; "no attribute values or identifiers" is accurate.
Fix: Replace the assertion with a short analysis: state leaf format, confirm that leaf pre-images include a random nonce or high-entropy receipt id (so that pre-image guessing is infeasible), set a minimum batch size or time window, and say that roots are expected not to be personal data for outsiders but may be for the operator, who is the controller anyway. Cite EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 (see references). Soften the abstract wording.

### P-3 MAJOR: DPDP Act treated as "GDPR-lite" and some statements overstated or missing the Indian specifics
Location: 01-introduction ("India's DPDP Act ... likewise ties processing to a specified purpose and requires erasure once that purpose is no longer served"); 02-background; 10-discussion first subsection.
Problem: The statement is roughly right (s.5 notice with purpose; s.6(1) consent limited to the necessary data for the specified purpose; s.8(7) erasure once purpose served or consent withdrawn, unless retention is required by law) but (a) the Act has no standalone "storage limitation"/"accountability" Article-5-style principle list; the mapping is by section, and the paper cites none; (b) the paper never uses DPDP vocabulary (Data Fiduciary, Data Processor, Data Principal, Consent Manager), although a campus is a Data Fiduciary and students are Data Principals; (c) under s.8(7)(b) the erasure duty extends to processors the fiduciary engaged, and s.8(2) makes the fiduciary responsible for processors, so a third-party "service" is a processor or an independent fiduciary and the declaration's deletion-date clause maps to this; the paper never classifies services; (d) s.4 needs a lawful ground: consent (s.6) or certain legitimate uses (s.7), and many institute operations (enrolment, exams) may rely on s.7 rather than consent, in which case the TrustTrail "consent screen" is a notice, not a consent; (e) s.9 requires verifiable parental consent for children under 18, and Indian undergraduates are frequently 17-18; (f) s.8(10) requires a grievance redressal mechanism and the DPDP Rules 2025 (notified Nov 2025) set response limits (up to 90 days) and the Rules' commencement is phased, so obligations may not be in force at submission; (g) s.8(5) "reasonable security safeguards" and s.8(6) breach notification are relevant to the operator's single-point-of-trust role but unmentioned. The paper also says the Act "requires erasure once the purpose is no longer served" without the s.8(7) exceptions (retention required by law), which matter for exam records and for the ledger.
Fix: Add a table mapping mechanism to provision for both regimes (see section 4) and name roles. State explicitly which lawful ground is assumed (consent vs s.7 legitimate use), and state that the Rules were notified in Nov 2025 with phased commencement and that the paper does not assess Rules-level obligations.

### P-4 MAJOR: Operator governance is one sentence for a design that concentrates power in the operator
Location: 10-discussion "What the operator can still do" ("A deployment would place TrustTrail's administration with an office that is independent of the services it oversees"); 05 Parties; 07 "trust_reset"; 10 "Key custody".
Problem: The operator (a) holds the identity map (de-anonymisation), (b) decides what enters the ledger (can silently omit), (c) can reset any trust score and thus restore a violating service's access, (d) holds receipt and anchoring keys, (e) runs the dashboard where the student authenticates with her real identity. The institution is simultaneously controller, the auditor of services and, often, itself a service operator (the examination portal is the institute's own system). "Independent office" is not defined; in an Indian institute there is typically no such office other than IT services/registrar. There is no separation of duties, no dual control on resets, no policy on who may query the identity map or for what, no logging of operator queries to the map (an insider can look up a student's pseudonyms without any trace in the ledger), and no student-facing complaint path. A trust_reset is only "recorded"; the student is not notified. This also sits badly with the claim in the abstract that edits "by the operator can be detected": detection is only of edits after entry, while omission and map abuse remain.
Fix: Add a governance subsection: roles (DPO/grievance officer, system administrator, auditor), two-person approval for resets and for identity-map access, ledger entries for every operator identity-map lookup and admin action, notification to affected students on reset, and periodic publication of anchors/trust summaries to the institute's data protection committee. Cite the grievance-officer role explicitly (it is already mentioned as a "verifier" in 05; extend this to a procedure). Say who inside a typical Indian institute (registrar, CIO, DPO equivalent) would hold each role.

### P-5 MAJOR: The "no-record, no-recourse" problem is only half solved: a student has evidence but no remedy
Location: 01-introduction ("No record and no recourse"); 10-discussion; 11 future work.
Problem: Receipts and dashboard give the student evidence, but there is no flow for the student to act on it: no complaint submission, no right-to-withdraw that propagates to a service (deletion request through TrustTrail), no linkage to s.13 DPDP grievance/Board complaint or GDPR Art. 77. The title promise of "recourse" is therefore not delivered. Also, student choice at release is deferred to future work (11 "Student choice at release"), so the student is not actually a decision maker: tiers are decided by the institute and TrustTrail. Consent in a student-institute relationship is also structurally weak (power imbalance, GDPR Recital 43), which the paper does not discuss; students cannot refuse the library or exam portal.
Fix: Either (a) add a minimal "request deletion / raise grievance" action on the dashboard that creates a ledger entry and a notice to the service, or (b) reword "recourse" to "evidence for recourse". Add a paragraph on why consent is a weak basis in this setting and that the paper treats TrustTrail as a transparency and enforcement layer independent of the legal basis.

### P-6 MAJOR: Usability study plan and ethics statement are inadequate and inconsistent
Location: 11-future-work "Usability study"; 13-backmatter "Ethics Statement".
Problem: (1) The Ethics Statement says "This work involved no human participants" while the same paragraph says a study with participants is planned; acceptable for now but the Future Work says "after checking whether the institute requires ethics approval". Approval should be obtained *before* design is finalised, not "checked". (2) Recruiting "15 to 20 classmates" is a convenience sample of people in a dependent relationship with the investigators (and, for a project report, perhaps with grades); voluntariness, anonymity and non-coercion are not addressed. (3) The study records decision time and choices; if participants log in with real student accounts to the Keycloak screens, personal data is processed and DPDP notice/consent (s.5-6) applies; the paper says an "invented student" persona is used, which is good, but responses plus demographics from small cohorts can be re-identifying. No data-management plan (storage, retention, deletion, who sees raw data). (4) Comprehension of a consent screen by classmates who know the authors and the system is a biased test; n of 15-20 with an exact sign test has little power and the paper should state the minimum detectable effect. (5) The within-subject design with the prototype screen having per-attribute checkboxes and Keycloak's screen not allows it makes comparison of "choice" invalid, which the paper concedes, but it also biases comprehension in favour of the prototype by design. (6) Nothing on debriefing or the right to withdraw data. (7) The study measures attribute-sharing choice but not whether students understand "trust tier" or "deletion date" in the Indian context (language, English-medium assumptions).
Fix: Rewrite the ethics paragraph: commit to institute ethics committee review (or an explicit exemption letter) before recruitment; written informed consent; voluntary and anonymous participation; no link to grades; data-minimised collection (no names, store only pseudonymous participant IDs); retention and deletion date for raw data; right to withdraw; report as an exploratory pilot, not evidence of usability. Fix the TODO placeholders (author contributions, repository URL) before submission.

### P-7 MAJOR: Practicality for an Indian institute is not argued
Location: 10-discussion "Scale and deployment"; 11 future work "Public-chain deployment"; 06-architecture.
Problem: (a) Who runs it: a single-institute deployment needs an operations team, key custody, a public-chain wallet funded with ETH, monitoring; no cost or staffing estimate (the 77,268 gas per anchor is reported but not converted to a recurring cost in INR/USD at some fee level). (b) Data localisation/cross-border: publishing roots on a global chain is not a personal-data transfer if roots are non-personal (see P-2) but this should be said; a global public chain also has no data-fiduciary relationship with the institute. (c) Keycloak in dev mode, Hardhat test keys: the paper says anchors "prove nothing to outsiders" at present, so the headline operator-resistance property is not demonstrated in a deployable setting; the legal-evidence value (e.g. admissibility of electronic records under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 s.63 certificate requirements, the successor to Evidence Act s.65B) is never discussed, yet "evidence" is the paper's key word. (d) No consideration of the Indian higher-education context: students under 18, regional-language notices, institutional reliance on third-party SaaS (e.g. Google/Microsoft logins, DigiLocker, ABC/APAAR IDs, the Academic Bank of Credits), which are the services most students actually use and which will not sign TrustTrail declarations. (e) The services in the evaluation are scripted by the authors, so the practicality of getting real third-party vendors to sign JWS declarations and respect deletion dates is untested.
Fix: Add a "Deployment in an Indian institute" paragraph: operating roles, an annual cost estimate for anchoring on a public chain or an L2, treatment of vendors under contract (processor agreements incorporate the declaration), evidence-law note, minors, and what to do for services that cannot or will not participate (fall back to anonymous/pseudonym profile).

### P-8 MINOR: "Purpose limitation" and "data minimisation" are supported only partly and the text sometimes implies more
Location: 02-background "Privacy principles"; 05 table G3; 07 declarations.
Problem: Declarations are checked by attribute name, not by purpose (the paper concedes this in 10 "Key custody"); the purpose is free text. That supports *recording* a purpose, not *limiting* use to it. Data minimisation is enforced at the attribute-name level against the service's base tier, set by the institute; there is no necessity test and no student role. The service could declare every attribute and "legitimately" obtain it. G3 uses the label "Use limitation" accurately, but the abstract and intro slide into "purpose limitation" language. Also "deletion date" is promised by the service but unverifiable (conceded).
Fix: Use the phrase "purpose and retention declaration, recorded and checked at disclosure" consistently; add one sentence that necessity assessment is a human governance step outside the system; consider a controlled purpose vocabulary (e.g. aligned to the W3C Data Privacy Vocabulary, DPV) so purposes are comparable and auditable.

### P-9 MINOR: Pairwise pseudonym claims vs. identity map risk and "anonymous" profile naming
Location: 10-discussion "Linkability that remains"; 07-design profiles.
Problem: The paper admits the "anonymous" profile still carries a pairwise sub. In legal terms it is pseudonymous at best, never "anonymous" (Recital 26); the profile name risks being read as anonymisation. The plaintext identity_map.db (all students x all services) is also a single re-identification table that is a high-value breach target (DPDP s.8(5), s.8(6)); encryption at rest and access control for it are not described.
Fix: Rename profile to "minimal" or "pseudonymous-minimal" in the paper (or explain in a footnote), and describe protection of identity_map.db and breach handling.

### P-10 MINOR: Special/sensitive-category and third-party-data edge cases
Location: 06-architecture attribute list (name, e-mail, roll number, department, programme, year, affiliation).
Problem: Not sensitive by GDPR Art. 9, but exam results/disciplinary status or caste/category/disability data (common in Indian admissions systems) could be added; the vocabulary is open to extension. Ledger entries recording access to such attributes would themselves reveal sensitive facts.
Fix: One sentence in the discussion on extending the attribute vocabulary and on excluding sensitive attributes from names in the ledger.

### P-11 MINOR: Citation quality of legal sources
Location: references.bib (gdpr2016, dpdp2023, kantarareceipt).
Problem: Only the whole instruments are cited; nothing at provision level, and no regulator guidance (EDPB blockchain guidelines, EDPB pseudonymisation guidelines 01/2025, Article 29 WP216 on anonymisation, Rules 2025). The Kantara entry links a wiki shortlink. The DPDP entry points to a MeitY upload; check that the document is the Gazette text.
Fix: Cite by article/section in text (e.g. GDPR Art. 5(1)(b),(c),(e), 5(2), 25, 17; DPDP ss.5-9, 8(7), 11-13), and add the guidance listed below.

## 6. Criterion judgements

- Legal accuracy: PARTLY_MEETS. The main statements are accurate and well hedged in 10-discussion, but the DPDP Act is described through GDPR categories without section references, the abstract overstates "no personal data on chain", and the ledger's own personal-data status is ignored.
- Practical relevance: PARTLY_MEETS. Addresses a real campus problem; deployment for an Indian institute (roles, cost, evidence law, minors, third-party vendors, dev-mode and test-chain caveats) is not substantiated.
- Ethical treatment: PARTLY_MEETS. Honest limits and synthetic data, but weak operator governance, no student remedy path, and an immature usability-study ethics plan.
- Stakeholder coverage: PARTLY_MEETS. Students, services, operator and verifier are modelled; the DPO/grievance officer, data processors, regulator (Data Protection Board), parents of minors and institute management are not.

## 7. Recommendation

MAJOR REVISION.

Rationale: the engineering contribution is credible and the paper is unusually candid about what it does not prove. However, for a paper that frames itself around GDPR/DPDP principles, the legal layer is thin and in one respect self-undermining: the tamper-proof ledger of pseudonymous access records has no retention or erasure design (P-1), and the claim that anchoring is privacy-neutral is not analysed (P-2). Operator governance (P-4), student remedies (P-5) and the usability-study ethics (P-6) need real content, not caveats. If the authors add a "Data protection of TrustTrail itself" section with a provision-level mapping for both regimes, a governance model, a ledger retention/crypto-shredding design, and a tightened ethics plan, I would support acceptance.
